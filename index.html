    Rancheras

    Singing rancheras with a deep voice

    Good news: many of the great ranchero voices are low and rich. Vicente Fernández, Javier Solís and Antonio Aguilar sang mostly in a baritone range. A deep voice fits this music well.

    The key rule: pick the right key

    Most songs can be moved to a lower key. In a karaoke app or on YouTube karaoke tracks, lower the key 1 to 3 steps until the highest note of the chorus feels strong but easy. Professionals do this all the time.

    What makes it sound ranchero

    • Long held notes at the end of phrases ("Volveeer, volveeer"). Practice with the "Long notes" exercise.
    • Open vowels: sing on "ah" and "oh" even when the word ends in a consonant, and add the consonant at the very end.
    • Chest voice with power: a full, speech-like sound. The power comes from your breath and body, not from tightening the neck. If your neck veins pop or your throat burns, back off.
    • Emotion and phrasing: rancheras are storytelling. Say the lyrics out loud like you mean them before singing.
    • Vibrato on long notes, which develops naturally with relaxed, supported singing.

    The grito, safely

    The mariachi grito ("ay ay ay!", "¡ajúa!") is a light, high, flipping sound, closer to a laugh or a yodel than a scream. Start quietly: a high "hee" that flips down to a lower "ah" like a happy laugh. Keep it light, do only a few per session, and never do it when your voice feels tired or hoarse.

    Beginner songs, easiest first

    1. El Rey (José Alfredo Jiménez): small range, steady rhythm, everyone knows it.
    2. Cielito Lindo (traditional): simple melody, great for breath control.
    3. Hermoso Cariño (Vicente Fernández): short phrases, fits a low voice.
    4. Volver, Volver (Vicente Fernández): the classic long held notes.
    5. Mujeres Divinas (Vicente Fernández): good low range with some power in the chorus.
    6. Sombras or Payaso (Javier Solís): bolero ranchero, for smooth, warm tone.

    Save for later: Amor Eterno and Si nos dejan. They're beautiful but sit high and need strong breath control. Lower the key when you try them.

    8-week plan

    8-week plan: from singing along to singing rancheras

    Built for about 20 to 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week. Short daily sessions beat one long session, for learning and for your vocal folds. Each session: 10 min warmup, 10 to 15 min focus skill, 5 min singing a ranchera you love.

    Weeks 1–2Body, breath and matching pitch
    • Posture: feet hip-width, knees soft, chest open without raising shoulders, head balanced. Mariachi singers stand tall and grounded; copy that.
    • Breathing: inhale low and wide so the belly and lower ribs expand; exhale on "sss" for 15, then 20, then 25 seconds, steady and even.
    • Practice on Deep voice, "Match notes". Goal: 10 matches per session.
    • Measure your range once and switch Practice to "My range".
    • Song: El Rey, just the verses, speaking the words in rhythm and then singing them quietly.
    Weeks 3–4Steady tone and scales
    • "Scales" exercise on "ah" and "oh".
    • Lip trills and straw humming every day (the best-studied safe exercises).
    • Start the "Long notes" exercise: hold 4 seconds with the dot steady in the middle. This is the core ranchera skill.
    • Song: Cielito Lindo all the way through.
    Weeks 5–6Power without pushing, and vowels
    • Sing long notes on the vowels rancheras love: "ay", "ah", "oh". Keep the jaw loose and the throat open, like a yawn starting.
    • Crescendo drill: start a long note soft, grow to strong, back to soft, staying on pitch. Volume comes from breath, never from squeezing the throat.
    • Sirens on "ng" up into your higher notes, lightly, to smooth the break.
    • Record yourself once a week on your phone and listen back.
    • Song: Volver, Volver (lower the key if the chorus is a strain).
    Weeks 7–8Style: vibrato, the grito and a full song
    • Gentle vibrato: on a long note, let the pitch wobble slightly and evenly. Don't force it; it appears on its own when the voice is free and the breath steady.
    • Grito practice (see the Rancheras tab), softly and only a few times per session.
    • Perform one complete song start to finish, recorded. Compare with week 1.
    • Re-measure your range. A few lessons with a teacher now will go a long way.

    Why this order

    • Most people who think they're "tone deaf" aren't. True amusia is rare (a few percent of people); pitch matching is a coordination skill that improves with practice and feedback.
    • Seeing your pitch on screen while you sing helps beginners correct faster, which is why Practice shows a moving dot and a pitch line.
    • Rancheras demand big, long, held notes. Those depend on steady breath first, so power and style come last.
    Voice health

    Keeping your voice healthy

    • Hydrate through the day; your vocal folds are kept moist from the inside, so it's water over hours, not a sip before singing. Steam inhalation helps in dry air.
    • Never push through pain, burning or a scratchy feeling. Stop, rest, try again tomorrow quieter.
    • Avoid shouting and long loud talking on practice days, and don't whisper forcefully when hoarse (it strains too).
    • Warm up and cool down: finish with a few gentle descending sighs or lip trills.
    • Sleep, reflux and smoking all affect the voice. Late heavy meals and alcohol before singing can make it worse.
    • See a doctor (ENT) if hoarseness lasts more than about two weeks, or if you lose your voice suddenly or feel pain when singing.
    Sound and microphone tips
    • Phone speakers can't play very low notes well. "Note: bright" adds overtones so you can hear them; "Note: octave up" plays the note one octave higher, and you sing it in your own low octave.
    • On iPhone, turn off silent mode (the side switch) and turn the volume up after tapping Start.
    • Headphones give the clearest reference note and the most accurate reading.
    • If the reading jumps around, sing a little louder on a steady "ah".
